# Culturally grounded, food-only recipe research

## Key Facts

### 1. Jute mallow / lalo (Corchorus olitorius)

* **Food use:** Tender leaves are used as a mucilaginous leafy vegetable in several West African cuisines. In Senegal, the Wolof name **lalo** is associated with a dried-leaf powder used to thicken or finish stews; names and recipes vary by household and country.
* **Practical recipe direction:** Wash fresh leaves, remove tough stems, chop, and simmer with onion, tomato/pepper, and a chosen protein or legume until tender. Dried leaf powder is added near the end so its texture is retained. This is a culinary template, not a single “authentic” recipe.
* **Safe preparation:** Use correctly identified food-market leaves; discard damaged material and wash under potable running water. Cook until tender. Do not present raw leaves, wild-harvested leaves, or an unfamiliar species as interchangeable with *C. olitorius*.
* **Regional variation:** Senegalese lalo preparations, Nigerian/Ewe-style green soups, and Sahelian leaf sauces differ in names, fat, aromatics, thickness, and whether fish, meat, or legumes are used. A recipe must name its region rather than call all versions “West African.”

### 2. Cowpea (Vigna unguiculata): akara and steamed bean pudding

* **Food use:** Cowpea is a major West African pulse. **Akara/akùrà** is a seasoned, fried cowpea batter; **moi moi/moin-moin** is a seasoned cowpea batter steamed in a wrapper or mold. Both are widely documented Nigerian and neighboring-region foods, with local names and methods.
* **Practical recipe direction:** Soak and dehulled cowpeas are ground with water, onion, and pepper; the batter is seasoned and either spoon-fried (akara) or steamed (moi moi). The species should be named on the ingredient card, while black-eyed pea is identified as a common market type rather than assumed to be the only type.
* **Safe preparation:** Soaking water should be discarded; use clean water and utensils. Batter must be cooked through. For frying, use a stable pan, avoid crowding, and follow ordinary hot-oil precautions. Steamed batter should reach a firm, fully set center. These are food-safety instructions, not medical advice.
* **Regional variation:** Akara and moi moi have many household, religious, and regional variants: plain, leaf-wrapped, molded, or made with fish/egg/vegetables. Do not attribute one garnish or wrapper to all West Africa.

### 3. Sweet-potato leaves (Ipomoea batatas)

* **Food use:** Young sweet-potato leaves and shoots are eaten as cooked greens in parts of tropical Africa and elsewhere. The edible leaves come from the food crop *I. batatas*; they should not be conflated with unrelated morning-glory species.
* **Practical recipe direction:** Strip coarse stems, wash thoroughly, blanch or sauté briefly, then simmer with onion, tomato, groundnut, or another locally appropriate sauce. Keep the recipe explicitly region-specific (for example, a Ghanaian or Sierra Leonean household-style variation) unless a source identifies the exact tradition.
* **Safe preparation:** Cook leaves rather than serving them as a raw salad; use cultivated, correctly identified leaves and potable water. Heat-sensitive nutrients are not a reason to make health claims or prescribe preparation.

### 4. African leafy amaranth (Amaranthus cruentus)

* **Food use:** Tender amaranth leaves are cooked as a vegetable in African cuisines. Local names and species identification vary, so an application entry should show the botanical name and avoid treating every plant called “green” or “amaranto” as the same species.
* **Practical recipe direction:** Pick over and wash tender leaves, blanch or simmer, and finish with onion, tomato, groundnut, or a small amount of oil. The flavor and texture can be presented as a green side or sauce rather than claiming a universal national dish.
* **Safe preparation:** Avoid unidentified wild amaranths; source cultivated food leaves, wash, and cook thoroughly. Do not repeat claims that amaranth treats disease or corrects deficiencies.

### 5. African eggplant / gboma (Solanum aethiopicum)

* **Food use:** *S. aethiopicum* fruits and, in some traditions, leaves are used as vegetables. “African eggplant” covers diverse forms and names; gboma is commonly associated with leafy preparations in parts of coastal West Africa.
* **Practical recipe direction:** Use firm, correctly identified fruits or market-sold leaves. Slice fruits into a tomato-onion stew, or simmer tender leaves as a green sauce, depending on the named regional tradition. Bitter and non-bitter cultivars differ.
* **Safe preparation:** Cook fruits or leaves; do not recommend raw wild Solanum material. Exclude damaged or very bitter specimens and do not imply that bitterness is medicinal. Species-level identification matters because Solanum plants are not interchangeable.

## Notable Claims Requiring Cross-Reference

1. The exact country and language community for “lalo,” “gboma,” and “amaranth greens” must be attached to every recipe. Names are not proof that two recipes are the same.
2. Sources disagree or are incomplete on whether particular *S. aethiopicum* cultivars use fruit, leaves, or both. Confirm the cultivar and edible plant part with a horticultural or culinary source.
3. Social videos and posts can document community testimony or a recipe lead, but they are not evidence for medicinal, nutritional-treatment, or safety claims. No such claims should enter Savoir.
4. “African” is too broad as a recipe origin label. Prefer country, region, or language community, and label adaptations as adaptations.

## Source Quality Assessment

* **Tier 1 — institutional/scientific:** FAO/INFOODS West African Food Composition Table; CGIAR/International Potato Center materials on sweet potato; World Vegetable Center materials on African eggplant; and peer-reviewed ethnobotanical or horticultural papers. These are appropriate for identity, food use, and cautious preparation context, but not automatically for every regional recipe.
* **Tier 2 — established culinary/horticultural references:** university extension and culinary-history sources that identify a recipe and its region. Useful for technique; cross-check botanical identity.
* **Tier 3 — public social posts:** leads and cultural/community testimony only. Record exact title, URL, platform, date, and visible snippet; do not copy text or infer facts when a post cannot be opened.
